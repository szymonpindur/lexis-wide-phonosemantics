Sound–meaning mappings in English and Polish.

How does language express meaning through its sound structure? This project investigates systematic associations between phonological form and semantic properties across English and Polish lexis—from individual speech sounds and articulatory features to recurring phoneme sequences.

The aim is to map sound–meaning relationships across whole lexicons, rather than only within a few familiar word clusters. Phonaesthemes such as gl- in glow, gleam and glitter illustrate the question: do words sharing a sound pattern also tend to share aspects of meaning? Computational modelling extends that question to thousands of words and 65 experiential semantic dimensions.

The project also examines the sources of these relationships. Shared morphemes and lexical history can connect form and meaning, while phonetic properties may support perceptual or conceptual analogies. The analysis compares sound–meaning patterns under broad morphological and structural adjustment, measuring how the recorded roots, affixes and recurring lexical constructions change coefficients, uncertainty and discoveries. Together, these comparisons help distinguish the contribution of larger compositional structures from associations at the level of sounds and sound sequences.

1. Semantic and lexical data

The semantic framework is Binder et al.’s 65 experiential attributes, covering sensory, motor, spatial, temporal, causal, social, cognitive, affective, motivational and attentional properties. The original taxonomy has 14 domains; the interface collects them into four display panels without changing any hypothesis. [1]

Fine-tuned transformer models predict 65 continuous semantic scores for target words in contextual sentences: BERT-large-cased for English and HerBERT-large-cased for Polish, each with a token-level 65-output regression head. [2] [3]

English contexts come from the One Billion Word Benchmark training texts; Polish contexts come from the PolEval 2018 language-modelling corpus. The clean datasets use occurrence-weighted means of retained contextual predictions grouped into dictionary-supported lemma/POS units. Genuine inflections are grouped; distinct derived lexemes remain separate. Surface forms contribute in proportion to their retained contextual occurrences, rather than through equally weighted form means.

A sentence record consists of its text and ordered rated-target payload; these preparation counts follow sentence-level checks and precede target-level exclusions. One sentence may contain several rated words. The final semantic means use the retained target-token ratings shown in the last column.

The AI review is organized by unique spelling, not lemma/POS row. A spelling with several parts of speech can occur in several analysis rows. This accounts for the difference between the English review count and the English dataset size.

The Polish supervision-preparation script pairs translated Binder target words and generated Polish training sentences with the original English Binder rating vectors. These are not independently collected Polish human norms. Independent language models and corpora can produce different measurement properties, even with matching output dimensions. The regression treats the resulting semantic scores and their empirical ranks as fixed; training, translation and prediction uncertainty are not propagated into the displayed standard errors.

Outcome within each language:
Y = 100 × (average-tie rank of the aggregated score − 0.5) / N

A coefficient of +7 pp is a seven-percentile-point adjusted contrast between carriers and noncarriers in this language/cohort. It is not a 7% change in meaning, and equal percentile units do not guarantee equal semantic calibration across languages.

Phonology and supplied morphology

American English pronunciations derive from the CMU pronunciation dictionary, converted to IPA using the project’s ARPABET-to-IPA feature inventory. English morphology uses the English Lexicon Project MorphSp analyses. Polish pronunciations derive from Polish Wiktionary through a Kaikki extract, normalized to the project’s Jassem-labelled IPA feature inventory; supplied Polish derivations use the updated MorphyNet Polish resource. English lemma mapping uses dictionary-supported forms and WordNet; Polish mapping uses Polimorf. [4] [5] [6] [7]

Phonemes, articulatory features and consecutive phoneme bigrams are binary occurrence indicators—not counts. A feature is the logical OR of its source-defined member phonemes. The feature inventory reflects the recorded coding, including diphthong membership in multiple categories; it should not be read as a universal phonetic ontology. The six maps here cover general vocabulary; onomatopoeia is not silently included as another analysed cohort.

2. Morphological and structural controls

The strategy deliberately offers a broad inventory of lexical regularities that might account for phonosemantic associations. It combines complementary sources rather than relying on a single morphological analysis:

  1. Dictionary-derived morphology: roots, components and derivational affixes from the supplied English and Polish lexical resources.
  2. Morfessor segmentation: an unsupervised probabilistic algorithm that learns recurring word segments from unannotated word data. Its objective balances a compact segment lexicon against how well those segments encode the observed wordforms. Recurring pieces can therefore be learned without a pre-specified morpheme dictionary. Here, retained initial/final segment indicators supplement dictionary coverage with data-driven lexical regularities. They comprise 219 English and 174 Polish carrier patterns. The historical selection requires at least two letters and support in at least 20 recorded historical families. These are statistically induced units, rather than exclusively expert-attested morphemes. [8]
  3. AI-assisted morphological review: existing analyses are reviewed and supplemented with components, normalized roots and affixes where further structure is identified. The workers were configured as gpt-6-luna with high reasoning. The review covered 26,156 English and 33,475 Polish unique spellings; annotations are linked back to the lemma/POS rows. Semantic ratings and association results were excluded from the annotation inputs.
  4. Word-boundary constructions: prefix/suffix indicators drawn from explicitly edge-marked dictionary and AI affixes plus recorded surface-audit literals. Both languages use the same matching rule, requiring a nonempty remainder; interfixes and unmarked roots are not turned into arbitrary substring indicators. This layer captures recurring word-edge structure beyond the annotated segmentations.

The additive design preserves every nonconstant distinct pattern from the original dictionary-plus-Morfessor and AI designs, then adds the boundary layer. Identical carrier columns are collapsed while retaining their source aliases. No new rarity or semantic filter is imposed on this union. The original Polish design also contains its recorded surface-affix audit layer.

A binary pattern is a distinct carrier vector over analysis rows, not a word, spelling or count of morphemes inside a word. If several source labels identify exactly the same carrier set, they share one column. The Polish–English difference of 12,952 columns consists of 1,592 more original patterns, 12,390 more additional patterns from the AI-enriched design and 1,030 fewer additional boundary patterns in Polish. Most of the gap therefore arises in the AI-enriched representation of the lexical inventory, rather than in dataset row count or the boundary extension. This is a measured inventory difference; it combines language structure, source coverage and annotation granularity. The downloadable inventory distinguishes new AI-annotation patterns from other patterns retained by that design.

Morfessor and boundary indicators make the adjustment broader: they allow recurrent form patterns to compete with the focal phonological predictor as explanations of its semantic association. This deliberately demanding design can also absorb phonestemic regularities; its purpose is to test persistence under broad structural adjustment. PDS determines which candidates enter each final regression. Adding candidates does not mechanically guarantee fewer discoveries, because selected controls can change both coefficients and standard errors.

Root-family grouping, used for clustered inference, is distinct from grouping inflections into lemma/POS rows. The recorded family assignments are held fixed across the control comparisons. Full-grid and panel comparisons track changes in coefficients, standard errors, selected controls and eligibility, separating annotation-pattern restoration from the additional boundary adjustment.

3. One predictor, one outcome, post-double selection

The scan was inspired by microbial genome-wide association methods, especially SEER/pyseer. Lees et al. (2018) fit a generalized linear model to each sequence element and include population-structure components as covariates. The phonosemantic analogue tests one phonological indicator against one semantic outcome, with lexical/morphological controls and forced covariates. The current implementation combines this one-target-at-a-time architecture with PDS selection and root-family clustered inference. [9]

For each phonological indicator D and semantic outcome Y:
1. Select controls that predict Y: Sᵧ (linear Lasso).
2. Select controls that predict D: Sᴅ (weighted logistic Lasso).
3. Fit Y ~ D + controls in (Sᵧ ∪ Sᴅ) + forced covariates.
   D is unpenalized in this final joint linear regression.

Double selection is designed to retain controls important for either the outcome or the focal predictor, rather than using only an outcome-selected set. Belloni, Chernozhukov and Hansen call this the “post-double-selection” method and study inference with high-dimensional, approximately sparse controls. [10]

Exact project choices: cᵧ=cᴅ=1.1; rigorous linear Y-side loadings with up to five post-Lasso residual updates and a final fixed-loading solve; D-side logistic loadings based on fixed-only one-pass rowwise scores. The final union is never replaced by an intersection or top-k cap. Forced covariates are the intercept, standardized phoneme length and POS indicators (noun reference; adjective, adverb and verb indicators). Other phonological targets are not all entered together, so a surviving phoneme coefficient is not a unique effect independent of every other sound.

The implementation adapts double selection to a continuous semantic outcome and a binary phonological indicator. The frozen specification records the loading routines, numerical convergence requirements and rank-aware projection checks used in fitting.

4. Eligibility, uncertainty and multiplicity

Targets are registered at a minimum minority prevalence of .004. Formal discovery eligibility uses the frozen .005 minority floor, D-side linear R² overlap limit .90 and exact-absorption rule. Minority means the smaller of the carrier/noncarrier groups. Exact absorption occurs when residual D sum of squares is at most max(10⁻¹², 10⁻¹² × D total SS). Full-union R² above .90 is a caution, not the same D-side exclusion. Ineligibility is lack of a permitted inferential contrast under these rules, not simply nonsignificance.

Final regression standard errors use root-family clustered CR1 to accommodate dependence among words assigned to the same recorded family; two-sided tests use a t reference with G−1 degrees of freedom. Displayed 95% confidence intervals are pointwise, not simultaneous intervals. [11]

BY-primary: global Benjamini–Yekutieli adjustment at q=.05 over the complete language-specific predictor × 65 grid: 24,895 English and 25,220 Polish pairings. BY accommodates arbitrary test dependence when its p-value assumptions hold. BH-exploratory: Benjamini–Hochberg at the same threshold and over the same grid, with weaker dependence requirements than BY. Ineligible cells enter multiplicity bookkeeping with p=1; their raw results/status remain distinct. No re-correction is performed by graph, predictor class or Binder panel. BY cannot fix biased or invalid marginal p-values. [12] [13]

BY-primary means the primary discovery rule within this displayed specification. The structural-control specification was revised in response to earlier diagnostics and frozen before its new fits; it is not preregistered. Historical results remain distinct. Count differences between languages are descriptive, not a formal test of linguistic systematicity.

5. Phonological feature composition and examples

Every registered phonological feature’s source-defined phoneme OR was checked against every frozen analysis row. The details show overlapping phoneme carrier percentages; sole-member rows; and an optional descriptive allocation that gives each feature-carrier row 1/k weight to each of its k distinct member phonemes. Allocated percentages sum to 100%, but are not coefficient shares. Outcome-band coverage uses observed ≥90th/≤10th percentiles. Separate phoneme coefficients are from their own main-scan models and selected controls—not a jointly estimated constituent decomposition. Exact carrier-vector equivalence is flagged explicitly.

Word examples use up to ten high and ten low carriers and noncarriers, with observed percentile scores. Selection deduplicates normalized spellings and recorded families, then uses a deterministic greedy maximin character-bigram Jaccard-distance heuristic on up to 128 lexically spread candidates. Bands are never widened to fill short groups. Orthographic diversity is not statistical independence. Hover shows at most five high carriers for a positive coefficient or low carriers for a negative coefficient; neither establishes adjusted influence.

X ordering is either complete-profile coefficient clustering (average linkage, Euclidean distance), alphabetical, or a fixed phonetic order with vowels first. Y ordering follows the verified Binder order or alphabetical order. These affect presentation only. Colours share the full saved ±21 pp scale; no arbitrary magnitude gate is applied.

Reading the results

The estimates describe sound–meaning relationships after adjustment for the assembled lexical structures. Control-design comparisons quantify how those relationships change under broader adjustment; phonological-feature composition shows which sounds carry each category. Interpretation combines effect magnitude, uncertainty, carrier examples and the semantic-rating procedure described above.
